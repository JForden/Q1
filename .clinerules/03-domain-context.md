# Domain Context & Key Terms

This document captures the specific terminology and narrative framing used 
throughout the textbook project. These definitions ensure consistency when 
discussing data, information, and the three-civilizations arc.

---

## Key Term Definitions

### Data
**Definition:** Raw symbols, measurements, or observations without inherent meaning.

Data is the unprocessed input — whether cave paintings, hieroglyphs, stock 
prices, or social media posts. On its own, data is inert. It requires 
interpretation.

**Example usage:** "The data sits silent until we interrogate it."

### Information
**Definition:** Data + structure + context + interpretation.

Information is what emerges when we apply rules, organization, and meaning 
to raw data. The same symbols can become different information depending on 
how they're structured.

**Example usage:** "Information — not raw data — is what ultimately drives decisions."

### Meaningful
**Definition:** What emerges when information is applied to decisions or understanding.

Meaning is not in the data itself but in how we use it. A dataset becomes 
meaningful when it informs action, reveals patterns, or answers a question 
we care about.

**Example usage:** "The data is identical. The structure transforms it into 
something meaningful."

### Answerable Question
**Definition:** A question that can be addressed with available data given 
three conditions: observability, specificity, and falsifiability.

A question is answerable only if the phenomenon can actually be measured, 
the question is precise enough to return a non-ambiguous result, and there 
exists some possible outcome that would prove it wrong. Questions that fail 
any of these conditions produce analysis that feels rigorous but isn't.

## The Three Civilizations Framing

The textbook traces data collection and interpretation across three distinct 
eras. This is the spine of Chapter 1 and recurs as a touchstone throughout 
the book. Do not resolve or conclude the third civilization early — the 
reader should feel the arc building.

### 1. Early Humans (Cave Paintings)
- **Time period:** ~68,000 years ago (Sulawesi findings, 2026)
- **Data type:** Handprints, animal silhouettes, simple symbols
- **Key characteristic:** Ambiguous, unstructured, intent unknown
- **Central question:** *Why* was this made? We have the data. The meaning 
  is gone forever.
- **Lesson:** Data without context is a guessing game. The cave painter had 
  perfect information. We have only the artifact.

### 2. Ancient Egyptians (Hieroglyphs)
- **Time period:** ~3,000 BCE onward
- **Data type:** Designed symbol systems with standardized rules
- **Key characteristic:** Structured, contextual, administrative and 
  religious use
- **Central insight:** The same glyph means different things depending on 
  placement and structure. Organization creates meaning.
- **Lesson:** Structure is not decoration. It is the mechanism by which 
  data becomes information.

### 3. Modern Era (The Digital Data Deluge)
- **Time period:** 21st century
- **Data type:** Sensors, social media, financial transactions, scientific 
  instruments, behavioral streams
- **Key characteristic:** Massive volume, velocity, and variety; continuous 
  and often invisible collection
- **Central challenge:** We are not short on data. We are short on the 
  wisdom to ask the right questions of it.
- **The uncomfortable truth:** The device was never the product. The 
  behavioral data it generates was. Most people never noticed the transition.

## Anchoring Examples

These are examples developed specifically for this textbook. They are 
Jack's. Do not replace them with generic alternatives.

### The THEOSTRICH Example
Used to demonstrate that grammar — not the symbols themselves — creates 
meaning. `THEOSTRICHCANNOTFLY` parsed as "The ostrich cannot fly" versus 
`THEO STRICH CAN NOT FLY` parsed as "Theo Strich cannot fly." The data is 
identical. A single structural rule changes everything.

### The 1929 Ticker Tape Example
Used to anchor the "what makes a question answerable" subsection. The 1929 
stock market ticker tape generated enormous volumes of price data. But the 
question "will the market crash?" was not answerable from that data alone — 
it failed specificity and falsifiability. The data existed. The right 
question did not. Grounds the three-criteria framework (observability, 
specificity, falsifiability) in something concrete and historical.

### The Google Flu Trends Failure
Used in the "Understanding the Problem" subsection. Google Flu Trends 
(2008) overestimated flu infections by nearly double during the 2012-2013 
season. The team asked "does search volume predict flu?" They never asked 
"does search volume measure illness, or awareness of illness?" These are 
not the same question. Named by researchers in *Science* as "big data 
hubris." The lesson: a bad question produces bad answers regardless of 
how much data you have.

### The Phone vs. The Data Stream
Used in the introduction to establish why companies collect data. A mobile 
phone is worth several hundred dollars. The continuous behavioral stream it 
produces — habits, movements, social graph, desires — is worth orders of 
magnitude more, aggregated across billions of users over years. The hardware 
was never really the product.

## Narrative Arc

The textbook follows this progression across chapters:

1. **Data is easy; meaning is hard.** Producing raw observations is 
   trivial. Interpreting them is where the real work begins.
2. **Structure creates meaning.** The same data, differently organized, 
   yields different information.
3. **Questions drive insight.** Data answers "what" and "when" reliably. 
   "Why" requires careful inference and human judgment.
4. **The right question is harder than the right answer.** Most analytical 
   failures are question failures, not data failures. Google Flu Trends had 
   good data. It had a bad question.
5. **We are the third civilization.** Our challenge is not scarcity of 
   data but abundance — and the discipline to use it honestly.
